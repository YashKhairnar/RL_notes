Finite Markov Decision Processes: Complete Chapter 3

Self-contained study guide for Sections 3.1–3.8 of Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (2nd edition) by Richard S. Sutton and Andrew G. Barto—written so every concept, symbol, derivation, and diagram stands on its own.

Actions now shape future states, not just immediate rewards.

In Chapter 2's multi-armed bandits, every action happened in the same single situation. In a Markov Decision Process (MDP), each action At produces both an immediate reward Rt+1 and a transition to a new state St+1. Because an action that looks bad right now might unlock high-reward states later, the agent must evaluate entire future trajectories—which value functions and Bellman equations make mathematically tractable.

3.1 The agent–environment interface

An MDP formalizes learning from interaction at discrete time steps t = 0, 1, 2, ... (which can represent fixed clock intervals or sequential decision points). At each step t:

  1. The agent receives a representation of the environment's state, St ∈ 𝒮.
  2. Based on St, the agent selects an action, At ∈ 𝒜(St).
  3. One step later, as a consequence of its action, the agent receives a numerical reward, Rt+1 ∈ ℛ ⊂ ℝ, and finds itself in a new state, St+1.

This creates a trajectory: S0, A0, R1, S1, A1, R2, S2, A2, R3, .... When 𝒮, 𝒜, and ℛ all have finite numbers of elements, the problem is a finite MDP.

Where is the agent–environment boundary?

A common misconception is that the boundary matches the physical body of a robot or organism. In RL, the rule is sharper: anything the agent cannot arbitrarily change belongs to the environment. Motors, mechanical linkages, sensors, and even the mechanism that computes rewards sit inside the environment—because the agent cannot rewrite its own reward generator at will. The boundary marks the limit of the agent's absolute control, not its physical skin.

The 4-argument dynamics function p and the Markov property

In a finite MDP, the random variables Rt and St have well-defined discrete probability distributions that depend only on the immediately preceding state and action:

p(s', r | s, a) ≐ Pr{ St = s', Rt = r | St−1 = s, At−1 = a } Eq. (3.2) · Joint probability over next state s' and reward r, normalized so Σs'∈𝒮 Σr∈ℛ p(s', r | s, a) = 1 for every (s, a)

What the Markov property means: Conditioning on (St−1, At−1) gives the exact same probability distribution as conditioning on the entire history (S0, A0, ..., St−1, At−1). In other words, the state St must be a sufficient statistic of the past—not because the environment literally forgets history, but because any relevant past detail (like velocity, not just position) must be encoded inside St.

Deriving transition probabilities and expected rewards from p

Because p(s', r | s, a) is the joint distribution of (s', r) given (s, a), all other environment summaries follow directly from basic probability:

  • State-transition probabilities p(s' | s, a): To find the probability of landing in s' regardless of which reward r is received, marginalize (sum) over all possible rewards r ∈ ℛ:
    p(s' | s, a) ≐ Pr{St = s' | St−1 = s, At−1 = a} = Σr ∈ ℛ p(s', r | s, a)
  • Expected reward for a state–action pair r(s, a): The marginal probability of receiving reward r is Σs' p(s', r | s, a). Multiplying each r by its probability and summing gives the expectation:
    r(s, a) ≐ 𝔼[Rt | St−1 = s, At−1 = a] = Σr ∈ ℛ r Σs' ∈ 𝒮 p(s', r | s, a)
  • Expected reward for a transition triple r(s, a, s'): By Bayes' rule, the conditional probability of r given (s, a, s') is p(s', r | s, a) / p(s' | s, a). Taking the expectation over r gives:
    r(s, a, s') ≐ 𝔼[Rt | St−1 = s, At−1 = a, St = s'] = Σr ∈ ℛ r · p(s', r | s, a) / p(s' | s, a)

3.2 Goals and rewards

In reinforcement learning, the agent's entire purpose is formalized as maximizing a single scalar number—the cumulative sum of rewards Rt. This might seem restrictive, but the Reward Hypothesis asserts it is universal:

"That all of what we mean by goals and purposes can be well thought of as the maximization of the expected value of the cumulative sum of a received scalar signal (called reward)."
— The Reward Hypothesis, Sutton & Barto (2nd ed.), Section 3.2

The golden rule of reward design—specify what, not how: The reward signal is how you tell the agent what you want achieved, not how to achieve it. If you give a chess-playing agent positive rewards for intermediate subgoals (like capturing opponent pieces), it may figure out how to farm captures while losing the game. Instead, reward only true goal attainment (+1 for winning, -1 for losing, 0 otherwise) and let the agent discover intermediate strategies on its own.

3.3 Returns and episodes

The agent does not try to maximize the immediate reward Rt+1—it maximizes the expected return Gt, a function of the entire future reward sequence Rt+1, Rt+2, Rt+3, ....

Episodic tasks (finite horizon T)

Interaction naturally breaks into subsequences called episodes (such as games or maze runs) that end in a special terminal state at step T, then reset to a starting state. Each episode is independent of the next.

Gt ≐ Rt+1 + Rt+2 + ··· + RT Eq. (3.7) · Undiscounted return for episodic tasks

Continuing tasks (T = ∞, γ ∈ [0, 1))

Interaction continues forever without resets. If we simply summed rewards to infinity, the return could diverge. Introducing a discount rate γ ∈ [0, 1) makes a reward k steps in the future worth only γk−1 times as much as an immediate reward.

Gt ≐ Σk=0∞ γk Rt+k+1 Eq. (3.8) · γ = 0 is purely myopic; γ → 1 is farsighted

Derivation 1: The recursive return identity

Notice that Gt+1 = Rt+2 + γRt+3 + γ2Rt+4 + ···. Factoring γ out of all terms after Rt+1 connects Gt directly to Gt+1:

Gt = Rt+1 + γRt+2 + γ2Rt+3 + γ3Rt+4 + ···
By definition of Gt in Eq. (3.8)
   = Rt+1 + γ(Rt+2 + γRt+3 + γ2Rt+4 + ···)
Factor γ out of every term from k = 1 onward
   = Rt+1 + γGt+1
Recognize the parenthesized sum as Gt+1 — Eq. (3.9)

Derivation 2: Why γ < 1 guarantees finite returns

Suppose every reward is bounded by some constant: |Rk| ≤ Rmax for all k. For 0 ≤ γ < 1, let S = Σk=0∞ γk = 1 + γ + γ2 + ···. Multiplying by γ gives γS = γ + γ2 + γ3 + ···, so subtracting yields S − γS = 1 &implies; S = 1 / (1 − γ). Therefore:

|Gt| ≤ Σk=0∞ γk |Rt+k+1| ≤ Rmax Σk=0∞ γk = Rmax / (1 − γ) < ∞ Because weights sum to 1/(1−γ), that quantity also acts as the agent's effective planning horizon

3.4 Unified notation for episodic and continuing tasks

Strictly speaking, episodic tasks need an extra subscript i for the episode number (e.g., St,i, Ti), while continuing tasks do not. Writing separate equations for both cases would be tedious.

The absorbing-state trick: Treat episode termination as entering a special absorbing state ST that transitions only to itself and generates only rewards of 0 forever. Because adding infinitely many zeros does not change the sum, both episodic and continuing tasks obey one formula:

Gt ≐ Σk=t+1T γk−t−1 Rk Eq. (3.11) · Works for episodic tasks (T < ∞, γ ≤ 1) and continuing tasks (T = ∞, γ < 1)—just not T = ∞ and γ = 1 together

3.5 Policies and value functions

A policy π is a mapping from states to probabilities of selecting each possible action: π(a | s) ≐ Pr{At = a | St = s}. Value functions answer "how good is it to be in state s (or take action a in s) if I follow policy π from now on?"

  • State-value function: vπ(s) ≐ 𝔼π[Gt | St = s] (value of terminal state is always 0).
  • Action-value function: qπ(s, a) ≐ 𝔼π[Gt | St = s, At = a] (take a once, then follow π).

Derivation 3: Bellman expectation equation for vπ(s)

The Bellman equation expresses the value of a state s in terms of the values of its possible successor states s'. Here is every algebraic step from first principles:

vπ(s) = 𝔼π[Gt | St = s]
1. Definition of vπ(s) — Eq. (3.12)
      = 𝔼π[Rt+1 + γGt+1 | St = s]
2. Substitute recursive return Gt = Rt+1 + γGt+1
      = Σa π(a | s) 𝔼π[Rt+1 + γGt+1 | St = s, At = a]
3. Law of total expectation: average over policy's action probabilities π(a | s)
      = Σa π(a | s) Σs', r p(s', r | s, a) 𝔼π[r + γGt+1 | St = s, At = a, St+1 = s', Rt+1 = r]
4. Law of total expectation: average over environment dynamics p(s', r | s, a)
      = Σa π(a | s) Σs', r p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ 𝔼π[Gt+1 | St+1 = s'] ]
5. Linearity of expectation + Markov property (future return Gt+1 depends only on St+1)
      = Σa π(a | s) Σs', r p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ vπ(s') ]
6. Recognize 𝔼π[Gt+1 | St+1 = s'] = vπ(s') — Bellman Eq. (3.14)

Derivation 4: Linking vπ and qπ + why vπ is unique

Look at step 3 above: the inner expectation 𝔼π[Rt+1 + γGt+1 | St = s, At = a] is literally qπ(s, a). That gives two exact bridge equations:

vπ(s) = Σa π(a | s) qπ(s, a)    and    qπ(s, a) = Σs', r p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ vπ(s') ] Substituting vπ(s') into qπ(s, a) yields qπ(s, a) = Σs', r p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ Σa' π(a' | s') qπ(s', a') ]

Why vπ is unique: For a fixed policy π, Eq. (3.14) is a system of |𝒮| linear equations in |𝒮| unknowns. In matrix notation, vπ = rπ + γ Pπ vπ, where rπs = Σa π(a | s) r(s, a) and Pπss' = Σa π(a | s) p(s' | s, a). Because Pπ has eigenvalues ≤ 1 and γ < 1, (I − γ Pπ) is always invertible, giving the unique solution vπ = (I − γ Pπ)−1 rπ.

3.6 Optimal policies and optimal value functions

We say policy π is better than or equal to π' (π ≥ π') if and only if vπ(s) ≥ vπ'(s) for every state s ∈ 𝒮. Why is this only a partial ordering? Because policy π1 might be better in state s1 while π2 is better in state s2.

Remarkably, in every finite MDP there is always at least one optimal policy π* that is ≥ all other policies in every state simultaneously. Multiple optimal policies may exist (when actions tie), but they all share the exact same optimal value functions: v*(s) ≐ maxπ vπ(s) and q*(s, a) ≐ maxπ qπ(s, a).

Derivation 5: Bellman optimality equations

Because an optimal policy π* must select an action that maximizes expected return in every state, v*(s) = maxa q*(s, a) (no averaging over π needed). Combining this with q*(s, a) = 𝔼[Rt+1 + γ v*(St+1) | St = s, At = a] gives:

v*(s) = maxa Σs', r p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ v*(s') ] Eq. (3.19) · Bellman optimality equation for v* (nonlinear because of maxa, so linear algebra cannot invert it directly)
q*(s, a) = Σs', r p(s', r | s, a) [ r + γ maxa' q*(s', a') ] Eq. (3.20) · Bellman optimality equation for q* (obtained by substituting v*(s') = maxa' q*(s', a'))

Why v* turns planning into 1-step search, and q* needs no model at all

If you know How to extract optimal policy π*(s) Model p(s', r | s, a) needed?
Optimal state value v*(s) One-step lookahead: arg maxa Σs', r p(s', r | s, a)[r + γ v*(s')] Yes
Optimal action value q*(s, a) Direct lookup: arg maxa q*(s, a) No

Because v* already summarizes the optimal future return from every successor state s', a greedy one-step lookahead is globally optimal. Knowing q* goes one step further: it caches the result of that one-step lookahead for every action a, so the agent can act optimally without knowing p.

3.7 Optimality and approximation

Although the Bellman optimality equations define v* and q* uniquely, solving them directly is rarely feasible. An exact solution requires three things that almost never hold simultaneously:

  1. Accurate environment dynamics: We must know p(s', r | s, a) exactly.
  2. Sufficient compute and memory: |𝒮| must be small enough to store a table and solve |𝒮| nonlinear equations.
  3. The Markov property: The state representation must capture all relevant history.

In real problems, |𝒮| is astronomically large, so we must use approximate parameterized functions rather than tables. Why online RL succeeds where exhaustive solvers fail: Online reinforcement learning naturally spends its approximation budget on frequently encountered states—making near-optimal decisions where the agent actually spends time while accepting larger errors on rare states that almost never occur.

3.8 Summary

Chapter 3 builds the mathematical foundation for the rest of the book: every subsequent algorithm (Dynamic Programming, Monte Carlo, Temporal-Difference, and Policy Gradients) is a concrete way of solving or approximating the Bellman equations defined here.

MDP Formalism

The dynamics p(s', r | s, a) encode one-step transitions and rewards under the Markov property. Rewards define what the goal is; γ sets the horizon.

Value & Bellman Equations

vπ and qπ satisfy linear consistency equations; v* and q* satisfy nonlinear optimality equations via max.

From Theory to Algorithms

Evaluating a fixed π is prediction; finding π* is control. Real problems require approximate, online methods weighted by state visitation.
