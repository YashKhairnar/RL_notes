Temporal-Difference Learning: Complete Chapter 6

Self-contained study guide for Sections 6.1–6.9 of Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (2nd edition) by Richard S. Sutton and Andrew G. Barto—the central idea of modern reinforcement learning.

Learn from raw experience like Monte Carlo, without waiting for the episode to end.

Temporal-Difference (TD) learning fuses the best of both worlds: like Monte Carlo, it learns directly from sampled experience with zero knowledge of the dynamics p; like Dynamic Programming, it bootstraps—updating an estimate based on other learned estimates after just a single step.

6.1 TD prediction

The core intuition (no waiting for the finish line): Imagine predicting how long your drive home will take. Monte Carlo makes a guess when you leave work (30 min), says nothing the whole drive, and only updates its guess after you park at home and see the final clock (43 min). TD(0) updates after every single block: if you hit a 5-minute traffic jam on the first street and from the next intersection it usually takes 35 minutes, your new estimate is immediately 5 + 35 = 40 minutes—so you adjust your guess for the first street right away instead of waiting until you get home.

In math, suppose we want to learn vπ(s) from experience under policy π. Constant-α Monte Carlo (from Chapter 5's return Gt and Chapter 2's step size α) has to wait until the episode ends to know Gt:

V(St) ← V(St) + α [ Gt − V(St) ] Eq. (6.1) · Constant-α MC: target is the actual complete return Gt (must wait until termination T)

TD(0) (one-step TD) updates V(St) on the very next step t + 1 by replacing the unknown Gt with one real reward Rt+1 plus the discounted guess at the next state γ V(St+1):

V(St) ← V(St) + α [ Rt+1 + γ V(St+1) − V(St) ] Eq. (6.2) · TD(0) update: move V(St) slightly toward the one-step target Rt+1 + γ V(St+1)

Reading the TD(0) formula piece by piece

  • Old guess — V(St): What you thought state St was worth before taking the step.
  • TD target — Rt+1 + γ V(St+1): Your updated, better-informed guess after seeing one real reward Rt+1 and landing in St+1.
  • TD error — δt ≐ Rt+1 + γ V(St+1) − V(St): The surprise. Positive δt means things went better than expected (so V(St) increases); negative δt means worse than expected.
  • Why it's an estimate for two reasons: MC only samples (it doesn't know the true expectation). DP only bootstraps (it plugs in V instead of true vπ). TD(0) does both: it samples one transition (Rt+1, St+1) and bootstraps from V(St+1).

TD Error δt and its exact link to Monte Carlo error

Define the TD error at step t as δt ≐ Rt+1 + γ V(St+1) − V(St) (measuring the difference between the new one-step estimate and the old estimate). If V is held fixed during an episode, the total MC error telescopes cleanly into a discounted sum of TD errors:

Gt − V(St) = Rt+1 + γGt+1 − V(St) + γV(St+1) − γV(St+1)
1. Substitute Gt = Rt+1 + γGt+1 and add/subtract γV(St+1)
            = δt + γ(Gt+1 − V(St+1))
2. Group δt = Rt+1 + γV(St+1) − V(St)
            = Σk=tT−1 γk−t δk
3. Unroll recursively until termination (where V(ST) = 0, GT = 0) — Eq. (6.6)
Algorithm: Tabular TD(0) for estimating vπ
Input: policy π to be evaluated, step size α ∈ (0, 1]
Initialize V(s) arbitrarily for all s ∈ 𝒮+, except V(terminal) = 0

Loop for each episode:
    Initialize S
    Loop for each step of episode:
        A ← action given by π for S
        Take action A, observe R, S'
        V(S) ← V(S) + α [ R + γ V(S') − V(S) ]
        S ← S'
    Until S is terminal

6.2–6.3 Advantages & optimality of TD(0)

Why TD beats DP & MC in practice (6.2): Unlike DP, TD needs no dynamics model p. Unlike MC, TD is fully online and incremental—it updates after every single step instead of waiting for episodes to finish, so it works effortlessly on continuing tasks and very long episodes.

Batch updating: MC vs. TD(0) converge to different answers (6.3)

Suppose you have a finite batch of experienced episodes and replay them repeatedly until values converge:

  • Batch MC converges to the sample average of observed returns—minimizing mean-squared error on the training set.
  • Batch TD(0) converges to the certainty-equivalence estimate: the exact value function of the maximum-likelihood Markov model built from the observed transitions and rewards. That is why TD(0) typically achieves lower error on future data—and computes it in O(n) rather than O(n2) memory and O(n3) compute!

6.4–6.6 TD control: Sarsa, Q-learning, Expected Sarsa

From prediction to control: Just as in Chapter 5, without a model we must estimate action values Q(s, a) rather than V(s) so we can pick arg maxa Q(s, a) directly. The three flagship TD control algorithms differ only in how they form the next-state target at St+1:

6.4 Sarsa (On-policy TD control)

Named after the quintuple (St, At, Rt+1, St+1, At+1), Sarsa samples the actual next action At+1 from the current behavior policy (e.g., ϵ-greedy):

Q(St, At) ← Q(St, At) + α [ Rt+1 + γ Q(St+1, At+1) − Q(St, At) ] Eq. (6.7) · Converges to q* if all (s, a) are visited infinitely often and the policy becomes greedy in the limit (GLIE)

6.5 Q-learning (Off-policy TD control)

Q-learning directly approximates q* regardless of the exploratory policy being followed, by taking maxa over the next state's actions (no importance sampling needed for 1-step!):

Q(St, At) ← Q(St, At) + α [ Rt+1 + γ maxa Q(St+1, a) − Q(St, At) ] Eq. (6.8) · Off-policy: learns the optimal greedy policy while behaving ϵ-greedily

6.6 Expected Sarsa

Instead of sampling one At+1 (Sarsa) or taking max (Q-learning), Expected Sarsa computes the exact expectation over the next action under π—eliminating variance from random action selection:

Q(St, At) ← Q(St, At) + α [ Rt+1 + γ Σa π(a | St+1) Q(St+1, a) − Q(St, At) ] Eq. (6.9) · Reduces to Q-learning whenever the target policy π is greedy 
Algorithm: Q-learning (off-policy TD control)
Initialize Q(s, a) arbitrarily, except Q(terminal, ·) = 0
Loop for each episode:
    Initialize S
    Loop for each step of episode:
        Choose A from S using policy derived from Q (e.g., ϵ-greedy)
        Take action A, observe R, S'
        Q(S, A) ← Q(S, A) + α [ R + γ maxa Q(S', a) − Q(S, A) ]
        S ← S'
    Until S is terminal

6.7 Maximization bias & Double Q-learning

The hidden trap in Q-learning: Suppose all actions in a state have true value 0, but noisy estimates Q(s, a) fluctuate above and below zero. The maximum of noisy estimates is almost always positive (𝔼[maxa Q(a)] ≥ maxa 𝔼[Q(a)])—creating systematic maximization bias.

Solution—Double Learning: Separate choosing the best action from evaluating it by maintaining two independent tables Q1 and Q2. On each step, flip a coin: with probability 0.5, use Q1 to pick the maximizing action and Q2 to evaluate it (otherwise swap Q1 and Q2):

Q1(S, A) ← Q1(S, A) + α [ R + γ Q2(S', arg maxa Q1(S', a)) − Q1(S, A) ] Eq. (6.10) · Because Q2 is independent of Q1, 𝔼[Q2(S', a*)] = q(S', a*) with zero maximization bias

6.8–6.9 Afterstates & summary

Afterstate value functions (6.8): In games like Tic-Tac-Toe or chess, we know the immediate deterministic result of our move (the afterstate) before the opponent responds. Evaluating V(afterstate) collapses many different (s, a) pairs that produce the same board into one entry, accelerating learning.
