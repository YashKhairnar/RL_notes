Deep Reinforcement Learning · Algorithm 2

Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)

A careful way to improve a policy: make the update useful, but keep the new policy close enough to the old one that the learning step remains trustworthy.

The whole idea

TRPO in one sentence

TRPO improves the policy while limiting how far the new policy is allowed to move from the old policy.

Policy gradients tell us which direction should improve expected return. But a step that is too large can destroy a policy that was already working. TRPO puts a “trust region” around the update.

Imagine walking downhill in fog: the gradient tells you which direction looks better, but TRPO prevents you from taking a giant step based on an uncertain local estimate.

Start with a concrete picture

What can go wrong?

Imagine that, in one state, the current policy chooses left 60% of the time and right 40% of the time. From the data it collected, the gradient says that right seems slightly better.

A normal policy-gradient update might make the new policy choose right 95% of the time. That sounds like progress, but the evidence may have been noisy. The agent may suddenly stop trying left, even though left was still useful in many situations.

Old policy

Left: 60%
Right: 40%

Aggressive update

Left: 5%
Right: 95%

TRPO asks for a more cautious change. It may move the policy to something like left 52%, right 48%—a real improvement, but still close enough to the policy that generated the data.

That is the central idea: the gradient chooses the direction; the trust region controls the size of the move.

TRPO’s three questions

What is the algorithm trying to do?

Question 1: Is this action useful?

The advantage Ât answers whether the chosen action was better or worse than the policy’s usual action in that state.

Question 2: How should the policy change?

The probability ratio rt(θ) measures how much more or less likely the new policy makes the observed action.

Question 3: Did we move too far?

KL divergence measures how different the new action distribution is from the old one.

TRPO’s answer

Increase the probability of useful actions, but only while the new policy remains close to the old policy.

Useful action signal + cautious policy movement = TRPO
Step 1

Why ordinary policy updates can fail

A policy gradient update has the form:

θnew = θold + α ∇θJ(θ)

This looks reasonable, but the learning rate α is only a rough guess. If the step is too small, learning is painfully slow. If it is too large, the new policy may behave very differently and perform much worse.

Small update

Usually safer, but may waste many samples and take a long time.

Large update

Can improve quickly, but may move into a policy that produces terrible data.

Step 2

What should the new policy optimize?

We want to estimate how well a candidate policy πθ would perform using experience collected by the old policy πold. TRPO uses a surrogate objective built from the probability ratio:

rt(θ) = πθ(At|St) / πold(At|St)

This ratio answers: how much more or less likely does the new policy make the action that was actually observed?

Ratio = 1

The new policy gives the observed action exactly the same probability.

Ratio > 1

The new policy makes the observed action more likely.

Ratio < 1

The new policy makes the observed action less likely.

L(θ) = Et[rt(θ) Ât]

Ât is the advantage. If an action was better than expected, increasing its probability improves the objective. If it was worse than expected, decreasing its probability improves the objective.

The surrogate objective estimates improvement without having to collect a completely new batch of experience for every candidate policy.

Step 3

The trust-region constraint

Maximizing the surrogate objective alone could make the policy ratio enormous. TRPO therefore adds a constraint: the new policy must remain close to the old policy.

maximizeθ Et[rt(θ) Ât]
subject to Et[KL(πold(·|St) || πθ(·|St))] ≤ δ

KL divergence measures how different two probability distributions are. The small number δ is the trust-region size.

A normal example: weather predictions

KL divergence compares a real distribution with an approximate model.

Real weather pattern P

Sunny
75%
Rainy
25%

Forecast model Q

Sunny
50%
Rainy
50%
DKL(P || Q) = 0.75 log(0.75/0.50) + 0.25 log(0.25/0.50) ≈ 0.131

The number is small because Q is imperfect but still gives some probability to both outcomes. If Q assigned probability zero to an outcome that P considers possible, KL would become infinite: Q could not explain an event that actually occurs.

Plain-English reading: how costly is it to use Q as an explanation for data generated according to P?

For discrete actions, the KL divergence from the old policy p to the new policy q is:

DKL(p || q) = Σa p(a) log[p(a) / q(a)]

In TRPO, p = πold(·|s) and q = πθ(·|s). We weight each log-ratio by how likely the old policy considered that action. The result is always non-negative, equals zero only when the distributions match, and is generally asymmetric: DKL(p || q) ≠ DKL(q || p).

Plain-English reading: how surprising would the old policy’s actions look if we used the new policy to explain them?

Small change

Old: [0.60, 0.40]
New: [0.52, 0.48]
DKL ≈ 0.013

Large change

Old: [0.60, 0.40]
New: [0.05, 0.95]
DKL ≈ 1.145

For every visited state, compare the old and new action probabilities. If the new policy changes those probabilities too much on average, the update is rejected or shortened.

KL is not asking whether the new policy is identical. It is asking whether the change is small enough that the old policy’s data is still a trustworthy guide.

TRPO’s promise: improve the policy objective, but do not change the policy distribution too much in one update.

Step 4

How TRPO solves the constrained problem

The exact constrained optimization problem is difficult for a neural network. TRPO makes a local approximation around the current parameters.

  1. Compute the policy-gradient direction of the surrogate objective.
  2. Approximate the KL constraint with a quadratic form involving the Fisher information matrix.
  3. Use conjugate gradient to approximately solve for the best safe direction.
  4. Perform a backtracking line search until the candidate update improves the objective and satisfies the KL limit.

The resulting step has the rough shape:

θnew = θold + β F−1g

Here g is the surrogate-objective gradient, F represents local curvature from the KL constraint, and β is chosen so the final step stays inside the trust region.

Why not calculate F−1 directly?

The Fisher matrix can be enormous for a neural network. TRPO only needs products such as Fv, which can be estimated with automatic differentiation. Conjugate gradient uses those products without explicitly storing or inverting the full matrix.

Put it together

The TRPO algorithm

  1. Collect trajectories using the current policy πold.
  2. Estimate returns and advantages.
  3. Compute the surrogate objective and its gradient.
  4. Use conjugate gradient to find a curvature-adjusted update direction.
  5. Scale the direction to respect the KL trust-region size.
  6. Use backtracking line search to accept a policy that improves the objective without violating the KL constraint.
  7. Set the accepted policy as the new old policy and repeat.

TRPO is policy gradient plus a safety rule: improve the policy, but reject updates that move it too far.
